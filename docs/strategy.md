# FoCo Social: where to take it

Research and decisions, October 2026. Sources are linked at the end of each section.

## The one-sentence product

**See which friends are at FoCo, and what's on.**

Dartmouth students already do this by hand: before a meal they text friends "foco? light or dark?" (the Dartmouth Folklore Archive records the habit across every class year). Eating together is how students socialize. In Sodexo's survey, 87% of college students named it the most typical way they see friends, and half worry about feeling lonely. FoCo Social turns that text into one tap, and turns the menu everyone checks anyway into the reason to open it.

## What the closest apps teach

| App | What happened | What it means for us |
|---|---|---|
| **Down to Lunch** (Stanford grads, 2015–16) | "Who's down to eat?" pings went viral campus to campus and hit #2 on the App Store, then died: mass invites to people you're not close to, spam as friends joined, and a stranger-danger rumor. Replies happened by text, not in the app. | The need is real. Default to small, deliberate audiences, keep "going soon" quiet unless you choose otherwise, stay friends-only. |
| **Google Who's Down, Hangski, Ufree** | Status-only "I'm free" apps keep dying: a status needs constant upkeep and gives nothing back when friends are quiet. | Presence has to cost nothing (automatic check-in), and the app needs value when no friends are around: the menu. |
| **Burbn → Instagram** | Burbn had check-ins, future plans with friends, points, and photos. It was "cluttered" and "overrun with features"; usage showed people only used photos, so they cut everything else. | We are a check-ins + plans app. Measure what students actually use before adding anything, and be willing to cut to the one thing. |
| **Foursquare → Swarm** | Manual check-ins needed games (mayorships, badges) to become habits; splitting the app in two broke the habit. | Manual check-in is a chore: lead with automatic. Keep the menu and the friends side in one app. |
| **Saturn, Snackpass** | Survived by pairing a daily utility (class schedule, food ordering) with a social layer; Snackpass reached 80% of Yale in a semester. | Utility first: the menu and dish alerts bring people back two or three times a day. |
| **Partiful** | The invite is the product; guests reply by text, without the app. It never tried to own the conversation. | No in-app chat. "Text about this plan" hands off to the group chats people already have. |
| **Zenly, Snap Map** | Loved for playful presence with strong privacy controls: off by default, mutual friends only. | Our auto check-in shares far less (only "at FoCo", only during meals, opt-in). Say so loudly. |
| **Howbout** | Feature-rich social calendar (plan pages with chats, polls, maps); its forced invite wall and privacy worries drew backlash. | Never force invites. |
| **Waitz / Occuspace** | Dining halls pay for sensors to show how busy they are. | Check-ins are a friends signal, not a crowd meter. Don't build a crowd meter until density makes it honest. |
| **Yik Yak** | Anonymous campus app shut down in 2017 over bullying. | Real names, friends only, report and block on every person: safety is a credibility feature. |

## What the consumer-app playbooks say

- **Fewer features, happier users.** MIT Sloan's "feature fatigue" experiments: people pick the 21-feature product in the store, then are less satisfied with it, and less likely to pick it again, than with the 7-feature one. Apple's own guidance: use the minimum number of tabs, since each one makes the app harder to learn. ([MIT Sloan](https://sloanreview.mit.edu/article/which-features-increase-customer-retention/), [Apple HIG: tab bars](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/tab-bars))
- **The next feature won't save you.** Andrew Chen: most people leave in their first few visits; new features mostly reach people who already stay. Fix the first visit instead. ([Next Feature Fallacy](https://andrewchen.com/the-next-feature-fallacy-the-fallacy-that-the-next-new-feature-will-suddenly-make-people-use-your-product/))
- **Get new people to the "aha" fast.** Facebook found that how many friends a new user had within a short window predicted whether they stayed ("7 friends in 10 days"). Our aha is seeing a friend at FoCo, or a friend tapping "I'm coming". That needs friends on day one. ([Chamath on Facebook's growth](https://chamatharchive.com/?p=608))
- **Latent demand, narrow launch.** Nikita Bier (tbh, Gas): find people doing something through a clunky process and simplify it; launch in one community (tbh reached 40% of one school in a day); show the core value in seconds; build for distracted use. ([Lessons from Nikita Bier](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-nikita-bier/))
- **Atomic networks.** Andrew Chen: the smallest group that makes the product work on its own (Tinder needed about 500 people per campus; Snackpass started with dorm mates). For us it's a friend group, a team, or a floor of 10–20 people who eat together. ([Lenny's: the atomic network](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/atomic-network))
- **Prompts at the right moment.** BJ Fogg: behavior happens when motivation, ability, and a prompt meet; make the action easier before trying to motivate. Mealtimes are the prompt; one-tap "Going soon" is the ability. ([Fogg Behavior Model](https://behaviormodel.org/))
- **Few, personal notifications.** About 10% of people turn notifications off at one a week, 37% at two to five a week; irrelevance is the top reason. People who get at least one relevant push stay far longer. Only send what a friend did. ([Braze](https://www.braze.com/resources/articles/opt-out-of-push-notifications-why-users-do-it), [Business of Apps](https://www.businessofapps.com/marketplace/push-notifications/research/push-notifications-statistics/))
- **One close-friends list.** Instagram shipped a single Close Friends list because "when there are multiple lists, people don't tend to use them." That matches our star. ([Fox 9 / AP on Close Friends](https://www.fox9.com/news/to-encourage-more-use-instagram-to-allow-sharing-with-fewer))
- **Chat, honestly.** Chat-vendor posts claim chat lifts retention, but the evidence for adding chat to a non-messaging app is weak, and 87–88% of US teens carry iPhones with iMessage group chats already. What chat did for retention (bringing people back) is covered by friends' notifications and the hand-off to Messages. ([Piper Sandler via MacTech](https://www.mactech.com/2025/04/09/survey-88-of-teens-currently-own-an-iphone-with-25-expecting-to-upgrade-to-an-iphone-17/amp/))

## What changed in the app (build 1.1)

**Cut**
- **Chats** (direct messages, group chats, plan chats, unread badges, message notifications). A plan's page has "Text about this plan" instead, which opens Messages with the plan written out.
- **The Alerts tab.** Food alerts live behind a bell on the menu (and in Me), and swiping right on a dish still follows it. Three tabs now: Menu · Together · Me, with Together in the middle.
- **Food requests and "Ask Dining".** The app promised requests reach Dartmouth Dining; Dining hasn't agreed, so the promise wasn't true. They come back as part of a Dining partnership.

**Simplified**
- One verb for joining a friend: **I'm coming** (was "On my way" on someone at FoCo and "Me too" on someone heading over).

**Changed for first-timers**
- **Signed out, the app opens on the menu**, with a "See which friends are at FoCo" row that leads to sign-in. Signed in, it opens on Together as before. Duolingo saw about 20% more daily actives after letting people try a lesson before signing up; Luke Wroblewski calls this gradual engagement. ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker/))
- **Notifications are asked for when they mean something**: on the "Add your friends" step ("Know when friends head to FoCo") and right after you post Going soon or a plan, not the moment Together opens. iOS only ever shows its prompt once, and social apps average about 39% opt-in; asking after a moment of value, with an explanation, is the consistent advice. ([Andrew Chen](https://andrewchen.com/why-people-are-turning-off-push/), [Lazyweb](https://www.lazyweb.com/research/percent-apps-priming-screen-before-ios-notification-prompt))

**Added**
- **"Add your friends" ends sign-up** (skippable). Together itself stays uncluttered, but a new student doesn't land on an empty screen. The same step offers the two switches that make Together work without effort, both off unless chosen: notifications from friends, and automatic check-in (status-only apps died because presence took effort).
- **Report or block anyone** by pressing and holding their name (App Store rule 1.2 needs a report option, and chat's report button went with chat). Reports arrive by email.
- **Feature counts** (migration 011): which features each person used on which day, nothing else. This is how Burbn learned to become Instagram, and how to check the benchmarks below.

**Kept, on purpose**: automatic check-in, the dark side / light side / upstairs picker, quiet-by-default going soon, one close-friends list, plans with Going / Maybe / Can't, groups as plan audiences (teams are natural atomic networks), invite by text, diet filters, the desserts strip.

## How to tell if it's working

Benchmarks from a16z for early social apps (run `usage_by_day` and `usage_weekly_retention` in the SQL Editor once 011 is live):

| | OK | Good | Great |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daily ÷ monthly actives | 25% | 40% | 50%+ |
| Day 1 / 7 / 30 retention | 50 / 35 / 20% | 60 / 40 / 25% | 70 / 50 / 30% |
| Week 1 / 4 (if weekly) | 40 / 20% | 55 / 30% | 75 / 50% |

A meal app has three natural prompts a day, so it should aim at the daily numbers. The retention curve should flatten between days 7 and 14. Also run Sean Ellis's survey after two weeks of real use ("How would you feel if you could no longer use FoCo Social?"); 40% "very disappointed" is the bar, and Superhuman's lesson is to look at the segment that uses the core most (here: people who use Together, not just the menu). About 40 answers is enough to see the direction. ([a16z benchmarks](https://a16z.com/do-you-have-lightning-in-a-bottle-how-to-benchmark-your-social-app/), [Superhuman's PMF engine](https://review.firstround.com/how-superhuman-built-an-engine-to-find-product-market-fit))

**Watch per feature** (from `usage_by_day`): if plans barely get used while going soon thrives, plans are the next thing to cut or fold into going soon. If the menu carries the app and Together doesn't, the social side needs a denser launch, not more features.

## How to launch it

Lenny Rachitsky's review of how the biggest consumer apps got their first 1,000 users found seven strategies; most companies used one, and none used more than three. For FoCo Social, two fit:

1. **In person, where the users are.** Table tents at FoCo with a QR code to the TestFlight or App Store link ("See which friends are here →"). Tinder started at USC houses; DoorDash with flyers at Stanford.
2. **One organizer at a time.** A team captain, a floor, a club, or a house adds their group and posts the next team dinner; everyone in it joins in one go (Partiful's campus pattern). Start with a few teams (dark side) and a few floors, not the whole school.

Keep it invite-only while it's on TestFlight: scarcity helped Clubhouse and Gmail. Don't pay for downloads, and never gate the app behind invites (Howbout's backlash).

## Next, in order

1. **Finish push notifications** (the Apple push key and the three Supabase secrets in the README). With chat gone, friends' notifications ("Priya is heading to FoCo", "Sam is coming") are what bring people back, and they don't reach anyone until this is done.
2. **Run migration 011**, update the App Store privacy label (product interaction → analytics), and ship build 1.1 to TestFlight.
3. **Seed two or three atomic networks** (a team, a floor, a friend group) and watch daily actives and day-7 retention for two weeks.
4. **Talk to Dartmouth Dining.** Official blessing is the biggest credibility multiplier (UK and WashU student apps were built with their dining services), the right home for food requests, and the path to using the D-Pine and the Dartmouth name.
5. **Only then consider new features**, starting with whatever the counts say people already do most. Ideas held back on purpose: a home-screen widget showing who's at FoCo (Locket's model), a mealtime notification when friends are already there, and a crowd meter once there are enough check-ins.
6. **Other campuses later.** Many colleges publish menus through Nutrislice, the same source the app uses, so the menu side travels; the social side has to be relaunched one campus at a time.
